# CLAUDECLARITY — Reader-First Writing

**The principle:** Write FOR the reader, not FROM your knowledge.

## The seven moves

Apply in order. Each move depends on the previous one being clean.

### 1. Subject discipline

State the subject in one sentence before writing. Then ask: is the doc *about that subject*, or am I drifting onto adjacent topics that are easier to write about?

Classic failure: writing about the speaker (Marc, the company, the credentials) when the subject is the reader's outcome. Credentials are *proof*, never *subject*.

If a section isn't directly the subject, demote it or cut it.

### 2. Reader-first composition

Pretend the cold reader is over your shoulder. Every sentence must land on first read. If a sentence requires knowledge the reader doesn't have, rewrite.

Practical test: read each line as if you've never heard of the project.

### 3. Capability framing over concept framing

Promises must be verbs the reader will be able to *do* — not nouns they'll *learn*.

- ❌ "You'll learn the Triad of Agents."
- ✅ "You'll be able to take a project too big for one conversation and still ship it."

Concept names belong in "what gets covered," not in "what you walk away with."

### 4. Concept-name translation

When a domain term must appear, translate it in plain reader-language in the same sentence.

- ❌ "Skills."
- ✅ "Skills — small, reusable instruction packs anyone can install."

Rule: a cold reader should glean the meaning without a glossary.

### 5. Verbatim preservation

When the user has already said it well in conversation, use their words. Don't paraphrase what's already clear. Paraphrase is where voice dies and academic prose creeps in.

If a line in the conversation could *be* the doc's elevator sentence, it probably should be.

### 6. Cruft detection

These look professional but make the doc worse:

- Metadata blocks ("Source," "Status," "Purpose")
- "How to update this document" sections
- Numbered subsections (2.1, 2.2) when a flat list works
- Status banners and version stamps
- Operations sections (traffic mechanics, KPIs) inside a definition doc
- Verbose "References" pointers
- Dates unless load-bearing

If a section doesn't directly serve the doc's one job, cut it.

### 7. One-screen budget

A definition doc should fit on one screen (~50 lines). Length is a forcing function — if you can't say it in one screen, you haven't understood it yet.

When the draft overflows: cut content, don't add headings.

## Acceptance test

Before claiming the doc is done, all answers must be yes:

- Does the first sentence state what the thing IS *for the reader*, not for the producer?
- Could a cold reader understand every promise on first read, with no glossary?
- Are all domain terms translated in the same sentence they appear in?
- Have you used the user's own words where they already said it well?
- Is everything that isn't the subject demoted or cut?
- Does it fit on one screen?
- Have you avoided metadata blocks, "how to update," status banners, and ops sections that don't belong?

If any answer is no, rewrite before showing the user.

## Anti-patterns

- *"It looked thorough."* Thorough usually means puffed up. Tightness signals understanding.
- *"I included it because it was true."* Truth isn't the bar. Subject-relevance is.
- *"I summarized the source."* Summary is the failure. Translation for the reader is the job.
- *"It's a working draft."* Every section still has to pass the cold-reader test, even in draft.

## Example: before / after

**Before** (puffed, speaker-centered, jargon-heavy):

> A free, live, end-to-end class on **how Marc Jovani uses AI to run a real $1.2M/yr business** — taught as one full arc from "what AI actually is" to "the building cycle that lets you create anything you can imagine."
>
> What the viewer walks away with:
> - A clear mental model of what AI actually is...
> - The **context window discipline** that separates an operator who ships from someone who burns afternoons in a chat that "got dumb."
> - The three levels AI works at (**Chat / Research / Build**)...
> - How to make AI behave a specific way using **Skills**...

**After** (subject-disciplined, reader-first, translated):

> A free, live class on how to actually use AI to build whatever you need to build — explained practically and concretely, with real experience from a working business.
>
> What you walk away able to do:
> - Build things with AI you couldn't build before.
> - Stop wasting hours in conversations with AI that go nowhere.
> - Catch AI when it's making things up — before it costs you.
> - Take an idea and ship it, alone, with AI doing the work of a team.

**What changed:** subject moved from Marc to the reader; promises became verbs the reader does, not concepts they learn; insider terms removed or translated; voice tightened; ~half the length.
